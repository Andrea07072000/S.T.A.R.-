Space and orbits

Orbital mechanics, time systems, reference frames, telemetry and space-safety data: the domain in which S.T.A.R. originated. Explore and configure real orbits in 3D, then review how the underlying software was verified.

Interactive · 3D · computed in your browser

Orbit design and manoeuvres in 3D: configure an orbit, apply a burn, observe the ground track.

Select a reference orbit or define your own parameters; the explorer reports period, ground-track shift and nodal regression in plain terms. Its mathematics is tested against our released packages and against two independent programs.

Model: two-body motion plus the secular effect of the Earth's oblateness (J2). Not included: atmospheric drag, solar and lunar gravity, the actual date (the Greenwich meridian starts under the first point). The mathematics is tested against the released S.T.A.R. packages star_elements, star_kepler, star_j2 and star_twobody, and against two independent programs: a numerical integration of the J2 equations of motion and SGP4 (198 checks; largest propagation difference 0.26 micrometres). The satellite is a symbol drawn far larger than real: its body follows the local orbital frame, its solar arrays turn towards the illustrative Sun, and its sensor cone (20 degrees, narrowed to the Earth's disk from high orbits) is illustrative (2581 checks on the 3D models).

Interactive · five missions, checked as you plan

Orbit missions

Plan a transfer, design a Sun-synchronous orbit, compare two satellites over a ground station, beat the Hohmann transfer with three burns, and catch the Moon. Every number is recomputed as you change an input, by tested mathematics.

Model: two-body motion plus the secular J2 drift, impulsive burns along the velocity, a spherical Earth for the transfer and the WGS-84 ellipsoid for the stations; no atmospheric drag, no Sun or Moon gravity, no refraction. Missions 4 and 5 are coplanar, with the Moon as a point on a circular orbit and its gravity ignored (the miss distance is measured where the craft crosses the Moon's orbit); they are checked against a separate numerical integration with the burns applied (scipy). Station coordinates are approximate public values (city centres; the Svalbard station plateau). The downlink rate is illustrative. The mathematics is checked against the released S.T.A.R. packages star_maneuver, star_j2, star_coverage and star_topo, a numerical integration of the motion (scipy), astropy's horizontal coordinates and a pass finder written separately in Python (659 checks).

Verification in detail: three programs propagate the same orbit

S.T.A.R.'s orbit propagator (a fixed-step RK4 integrator, star_orbit 0.2.0) was compared with two reference integrators on four test orbits. The two references agree with each other within the 0.1 mm precision of the recorded positions; S.T.A.R. differs from them by about 1.5 cm after one orbit, within the 5 cm tolerance and measurably the least precise of the three. star_orbit is VERIFIED, not yet RELEASED.
