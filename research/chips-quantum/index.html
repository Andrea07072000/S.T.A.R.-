Chips and quantum

What CPUs, GPUs, AI accelerators and quantum processors are really good at — shown in 3D, with one real measurement on our laptop and every simulation labelled as such.

Interactive · 3D

Four kinds of processor: what each one is good at

The same job, multiplying two square matrices, on three classical architectures under an ideal peak model you can edit, and a quantum processor that does a different kind of work. Every picture is labelled: a teaching illustration, a numerical simulation, or a measurement on real hardware.

CPU

How it works
A few powerful cores that each follow their own list of instructions; fast at decisions and branching.
Good at
Varied work full of decisions: almost every program, including all 70 S.T.A.R. packages and the Workspace.
Weak at
Huge amounts of identical arithmetic: it does it a few pieces at a time.
In S.T.A.R. today
Everything S.T.A.R. ships runs on ordinary CPUs: your laptop is enough.

GPU

How it works
Thousands of simple lanes that run the same instruction on different data at the same moment.
Good at
Large uniform calculations: graphics, simulations on grids, training neural networks.
Weak at
Work with many branches, small problems (starting the GPU costs time), data that must travel to and from its memory.
In S.T.A.R. today
Not used by S.T.A.R. today: none of our calculations needs one.

Systolic array

How it works
A grid of multiply-and-add cells that pass numbers to their neighbours in step, like a heartbeat — the core of many AI accelerators.
Good at
Multiplying large matrices again and again with little memory traffic.
Weak at
Anything that is not a matrix product; small matrices (the grid must fill and drain).
In S.T.A.R. today
A research line only: chip-design questions are hypotheses in our lab, with open tools installed and tried on a toy circuit.

Quantum processor

How it works
Qubits changed by gates and read by repeated measurement; results are probabilities.
Good at
In theory, specific problems such as simulating molecules or breaking some codes — at sizes no machine reaches today without errors.
Weak at
Ordinary arithmetic, large data, anything that needs exact repeatable answers. Today's devices are small and noisy.
In S.T.A.R. today
Results shown come from simulators (Stim, Mitiq). A few small test circuits also ran on IBM cloud processors through public access (October 2026, about 100 s in all); they are recorded, not presented as results. No quantum advantage claimed.

The classical times are best cases — the shortest each chip could take, with memory never waiting and nothing spent on starting the job (in the example most GPU lanes are idle: the table is small); real programs take longer (compare the measurement on our laptop). No figure here is a measurement of a commercial product.

The research status

  • Chip design: placement and floorplanning questions are hypotheses; open tools (Yosys, Verilator, Icarus Verilog, cocotb) are installed and were checked on a toy circuit: a 4-bit adder, all 512 input cases simulated correctly, 20 cells after synthesis (19 October 2026).
  • Quantum: Results shown come from simulators (Stim, Mitiq). A few small test circuits also ran on IBM cloud processors through public access (October 2026, about 100 s in all); they are recorded, not presented as results. No quantum advantage claimed.
  • Everything S.T.A.R. ships runs on ordinary CPUs.

No quantum advantage is assumed anywhere on this site. Where a quantum method is studied, it is compared with the best classical method, and the classical one is the default.
