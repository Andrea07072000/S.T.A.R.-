Four computing architectures: operating domains compared

The same workload, the product of two square matrices, on three classical architectures under an editable ideal-peak model, alongside a quantum processor that performs a different class of computation. Every view is labelled as a teaching illustration, a numerical simulation or a measurement on real hardware.

CPU

Operating principle
A small number of powerful cores, each executing its own instruction stream; efficient at decisions and branching.
Strengths
General-purpose workloads with frequent decisions: almost every program, including all 70 S.T.A.R. packages and the Workspace.
Limitations
Very large volumes of identical arithmetic: it processes them a few elements at a time.
Use in S.T.A.R. today
Everything S.T.A.R. ships runs on standard CPUs: a laptop is sufficient.

GPU

Operating principle
Thousands of simple lanes executing the same instruction on different data simultaneously.
Strengths
Large uniform computations: graphics, grid-based simulations, neural network training.
Limitations
Branch-heavy code, small problems (kernel launch has a fixed cost), data that must be transferred to and from device memory.
Use in S.T.A.R. today
Not used by S.T.A.R. today: none of our computations requires one.

Systolic array

Operating principle
A grid of multiply-accumulate cells that pass operands to their neighbours in lockstep; the core of many AI accelerators.
Strengths
Repeated multiplication of large matrices with minimal memory traffic.
Limitations
Anything other than matrix products; small matrices (the grid must fill and drain).
Use in S.T.A.R. today
A research line only: chip-design questions are hypotheses in our lab, with open tools installed and verified on a minimal test circuit.

Quantum processor

Operating principle
Qubits transformed by gates and read through repeated measurement; results are probabilities.
Strengths
In principle, specific problems such as molecular simulation or breaking some cryptographic codes, at sizes no machine reaches today without errors.
Limitations
Ordinary arithmetic, large data volumes, anything requiring exact and repeatable answers. Current devices are small and noisy.
Use in S.T.A.R. today
Results shown come from simulators (Stim, Mitiq). A few small test circuits also ran on IBM cloud processors through public access (October 2026, about 100 s in total); they are recorded, not presented as results. No quantum advantage is claimed.

Classical times are lower bounds: the minimum each processor could achieve, with no memory stalls and no launch overhead (in the example most GPU lanes are idle because the matrices are small); real programs take longer (compare the measurement on our laptop). No figure here is a measurement of a commercial product.

Where processors lose time

Peak speed is rarely the limit. Classify kernels on the roofline, find the size at which a GPU starts to pay for its launch and copy, measure what rare branches cost a 32-lane warp, and meet a time and energy budget.

Declared model, not a measurement: time = launch + copy over the host link + the longer of computing at peak and moving the minimum bytes at full bandwidth; energy = rated power × time. The profiles are the race above with stated memory, links and power (CPU 160 GFLOP/s, 34.1 GB/s, 15 W; GPU 6.1 TFLOP/s, 448 GB/s, PCIe 3.0 ×16, 150 W; systolic array 512 GFLOP/s, 68 GB/s, 5 W). Caches, clock changes and overlap of copy and compute are not modelled. Checked by 785 tests: hand arithmetic, a scan of every size, the binomial sum and a Monte Carlo of 200,000 warps.

Run a circuit simulation: voltage drop on a power grid

A small reference power-delivery mesh, 64 to 400 nodes, is solved exactly, then solved again after resistors are removed by effective-resistance sampling. You see what was removed, what it cost in accuracy and how long each step took on your device, and you can download the report with SHA-256 fingerprints of the inputs and outputs.

Reference implementation: not a sign-off tool and not a port of any published engine. It computes the DC operating point of the resistive mesh (capacitances do not change it): conductances from 1 to 3 S between neighbours, a load of 0.5 to 1.5 mA at every node, five supply pads at 1 V through 100 S, all drawn from a fixed generator (mulberry32, seed 1). Exact solve: banded Cholesky factorisation. Sparsifier: effective-resistance sampling (Spielman and Srivastava, 2008) on a spanning-tree backbone; its expected matrix is the original one, but the error bound of the paper is not claimed for this combination. A mesh is already sparse, about two resistors per node: this task measures what each removed resistor costs, it does not show a speed-up. Checked by 151 checks (tests/irgrid.test.ts: two 3-node networks solved by hand, a dense elimination, Kirchhoff's law at every node, Foster's theorem) and by a test that the Python script gives the same report on the same inputs.

Quantum circuits

Compose a circuit of up to three qubits, inspect the amplitudes and the Bloch vectors, add depolarizing noise and sample shots. Three challenges check what you build.

Exact arithmetic on state vectors and density matrices of up to three qubits; noise is the depolarizing channel of strength p after each gate on the qubits it acts on, as defined by Qiskit Aer. No quantum hardware is used and no quantum advantage is claimed. Checked by 826 tests against Qiskit's state vectors (180 random circuits), Qiskit Aer's noisy density matrices (180 cases and the Bell pair at 21 noise levels) and an exhaustive search for the fewest gates.

Research status

  • Chip design: placement and floorplanning questions are hypotheses; open tools (Yosys, Verilator, Icarus Verilog, cocotb) are installed and were verified on a minimal test circuit: a 4-bit adder, all 512 input cases simulated correctly, 20 cells after synthesis (19 October 2026).
  • Quantum: results shown come from simulators (Stim, Mitiq). A few small test circuits also ran on IBM cloud processors through public access (October 2026, about 100 s in total); they are recorded, not presented as results. No quantum advantage is claimed.
  • Everything S.T.A.R. ships runs on standard CPUs.

No quantum advantage is assumed anywhere on this site. Where a quantum method is studied, it is compared with the best classical method, and the classical method remains the default.
